_The first of five reflections from the "Broader Sectoral Perspective" module of the FMSF Leadership Development Programme, led by Gagan Sethi of Alliance for Change in February 2026._

Most leadership training starts with the organisation. This one started with us – and with a question I have not stopped thinking about: **how much of the way I lead was shaped long before I ever led anything?**

## Our story walks into every room with us

We began by introducing ourselves through the eyes of our mothers – what they were proud of, what they hoped for, and one thing they wished they had done differently.

It is a simple exercise, but it strips away the professional introduction in seconds. What is left is the person underneath the job title. That person is who our teams actually meet.

## Family teaches us belonging – and discrimination

Family and kinship give us identity, belonging and support. But one line from the session has stayed with me:

<Highlight>It is in the family that we also learn <em>obedience, violence and discrimination.</em></Highlight>

In development work we talk a lot about changing norms "out there". This was a reminder that the same norms live in us – who spoke at home, whose education mattered, how caste, gender and religion were discussed. They shape how we lead, often without our noticing.

## Draw the mountain of your life

The heart of the module was drawing our life as a mountain range, from birth to today, and marking the moments that made us: when we were held up, when we hit our lowest point, the risks we took, our peaks – and one contradiction we still find hard to live with.

<LifeMountain caption="The mountain of my life: from C1, birth, to C5, today – with the moments that shaped the climb." />

What struck me was how few of the important moments were achievements. Many were compromises, or people who quietly held us up. Our leadership is built on moments nobody else sees.

<Highlight tone="light" label="The line I took home">To be historic, <em>you need to embrace your history.</em></Highlight>

## A value is what you will pay a price for

The most useful definition of the week was the simplest. A value is **that which you are willing to pay a price for.**

Organisations are full of value statements nobody has ever paid a price for. Our behaviour – not our words – shows what we really value. The module even asked us to name our **"dirty" values**: the ones we act on but would rather not admit, like the need for approval or the fear of conflict. Naming them is the first step to not being run by them.

That teaching is a good test for every value we inherit – from our families, our organisations and our sector. Keep what is good for everyone. Let go of the rest.

## The questions I am carrying

- What on my mountain still shapes how I react under pressure?
- Which of my stated values have I actually paid a price for?
- Which "dirty" value is quietly running my decisions?
