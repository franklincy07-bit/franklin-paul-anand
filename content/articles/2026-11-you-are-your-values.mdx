_This is the first of five reflections from the "Broader Sectoral Perspective" module of the FMSF Leadership Development Programme, led by Gagan Sethi of Alliance for Change in February 2026._

Most leadership training starts with the organisation: strategy, structure, results. This module started somewhere far more uncomfortable – with us.

Over four days, Gagan Sethi kept returning to one idea. Before we can lead institutions, or try to change a society, we have to understand the forces that shaped us. Our history is not a private matter we leave at the door. It walks into every meeting with us.

## Introducing yourself as your mother would

The first exercise set the tone. Instead of introducing ourselves, we had to **become our mother**, or the mother figure in our life, and introduce ourselves through her eyes:

- What of me is in this person?
- What am I proud of?
- What do I expect from them in the future?
- And one thing I got wrong in bringing them up – and am sorry for.

It is a simple instruction with a deep effect. Speaking as someone who loves you, and who knows where they went wrong with you, strips away the polished professional introduction. It reminded me how much of who I am as a leader was formed long before any job title.

## Family: where we learn belonging – and discrimination

The module then looked at **kinship**: the networks of family and community that hold most of our lives together. Kinship gives us identity, belonging, support and a sense of where we come from. It sets out roles and obligations, and it connects us to wider social networks.

But the slide that stayed with me was a single line under all of that:

<Highlight>It is in the family that we also learn <em>obedience, violence and discrimination.</em></Highlight>

As development professionals we often talk about changing norms "out there" – in communities, in institutions, in young people's lives. This was a reminder that the same norms live in us. Who was allowed to speak at home, whose education mattered, how caste, gender and religion were talked about at the dinner table: all of it shapes how we lead, often without our noticing.

It is harder than it sounds. Some of the most important moments on the mountain are not achievements at all. They are the times we compromised, or the people who quietly held us up. Sharing those stories in small groups – and hearing others' – made it clear how much of our leadership is built on moments nobody sees.

The facilitator's line for this exercise has become one of my favourites: **to be historic, you need to embrace your history.**

## What a value really is

From the stories, each group had to name the values that had been **upheld, compromised or in conflict** with each other – and how we had reacted in each situation.

That is where the most useful definition of the four days came in. A value is:

<Highlight tone="light" label="A working definition">That which you are <em>willing to pay a price for.</em></Highlight>

It sounds simple, but it cuts through a lot. Organisations are full of value statements – integrity, inclusion, respect – that nobody has ever paid a price for. Our behaviour reveals our real values, not our words. And our present values are the result of our whole life so far.

The module also asked us to separate our **real values** from what it called our **"dirty" values** – the ones we act on but would rather not admit: the need for approval, the fear of conflict, the comfort of status. And to name our **value influencers**: the people who shaped what we care about.

<Sidenote icon="info" label="A thought to keep" title="Believe nothing merely because you have been told it">

One of the quotes shared during the session, attributed to the Buddha, says: _believe nothing merely because you have been told it, or because it is traditional, or because you yourself have imagined it. Do not believe what your teacher tells you merely out of respect for the teacher._ Whatever, after examination, you find to be good for all beings – hold on to that.

It is a useful test for any value we inherited, including the ones from our families, our organisations and our sector.

</Sidenote>

## Fear, guilt and shame

The last part of this first day introduced what the facilitator called **"the nine words which govern us"**: fear, guilt and shame; power over, power under and power with; and the three roles of the drama triangle – persecutor, rescuer and victim.

These words describe the hidden currents in most relationships, at home and at work. I will come back to power and the drama triangle in my next reflection, because they changed how I look at my own organisation.

## What I am taking with me

- **Start with yourself.** If I want young people and teams to examine their assumptions, I have to examine mine – including the ones I learned at home.
- **Test values by their price.** When we say something matters, the question is: what are we willing to give up for it?
- **Honour other people's history.** Everyone in a team carries a mountain of their own. Leadership is partly about making space for those stories to be told safely – with deep listening, confidentiality and no judgement, which were the very first norms we agreed as a group.
