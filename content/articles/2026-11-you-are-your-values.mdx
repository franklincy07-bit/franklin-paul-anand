_Part 1 of 5 in a series of my reflections from a leadership training I attended – the FMSF Leadership Development Programme, February 2026._

Most leadership training starts with the organisation. This one started with us – and with a question I have not stopped thinking about: **how much of the way I lead was shaped long before I ever led anything?**

## Our story walks into every room with us

Try introducing yourself through the eyes of your mother – what she is proud of, what she hopes for, and one thing she wishes she had done differently.

It is a simple exercise, but it strips away the professional introduction in seconds. What is left is the person underneath the job title. That person is who our teams actually meet.

## Family teaches us belonging – and discrimination

Family and kinship give us identity, belonging and support. But there is a line I keep returning to:

<Highlight>It is in the family that we also learn <em>obedience, violence and discrimination.</em></Highlight>

In development work we talk a lot about changing norms "out there". This was a reminder that the same norms live in us – who spoke at home, whose education mattered, how caste, gender and religion were discussed. They shape how we lead, often without our noticing.

## Draw the mountain of your life

One of the most powerful exercises I know is to draw your life as a mountain range, from birth to today, and marking the moments that made us: when we were held up, when we hit our lowest point, the risks we took, our peaks – and one contradiction we still find hard to live with.

<LifeMountain caption="The mountain of my life: from C1, birth, to C5, today – with the moments that shaped the climb." />

What struck me was how few of the important moments were achievements. Many were compromises, or people who quietly held us up. Our leadership is built on moments nobody else sees.

<Highlight tone="light" label="The line I took home">To be historic, <em>you need to embrace your history.</em></Highlight>

## A value is what you will pay a price for

The most useful definition of a value I know is also the simplest. A value is **that which you are willing to pay a price for.**

Organisations are full of value statements nobody has ever paid a price for. Our behaviour – not our words – shows what we really value. It is worth naming our **"dirty" values** too: the ones we act on but would rather not admit, like the need for approval or the fear of conflict. Naming them is the first step to not being run by them.

<Photo

That teaching is a good test for every value we inherit – from our families, our organisations and our sector. Keep what is good for everyone. Let go of the rest.

## Questions worth asking

**For yourself**

- What on your mountain still shapes how you react under pressure?
- Which of your stated values have you actually paid a price for?
- Which "dirty" value is quietly running your decisions?

**For your team**

- Which of your organisation's values has anyone ever paid a price for?
- Do you know the stories that shaped the people you work with?
