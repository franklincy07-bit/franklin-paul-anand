_Part 4 of 5 in a series of my reflections, drawing on Gagan Sethi's "Broader Sectoral Perspective" module (Alliance for Change) on the FMSF Leadership Development Programme, February 2026._

Most of us in civil society are trained to deliver projects. We design them, run them, report on them and start the next one. The harder question is this: **is any of it adding up to lasting change in people's lives, beyond the life of the project?**

## A world under strain

It starts with a sober look at the world we work in today:

- **Weakening institutional values.** Competition for resources is getting acute, and trust in institutions of every kind – including civil society itself – is wearing thin. In a word, _aitbaar_ – trust itself – is under strain.
- **Core values under stress.** Basic humanity – _insaniyat_ – is being pushed aside. "To be" has become "to have".
- **Conflict and inequality.** The paradox of poverty amid plenty, widening gaps between people, and too little space for people to be themselves.

<Sidenote icon="info" label="A story worth knowing" title="The truth coming out of the well">

When I think about misinformation, I think of a 19th-century legend behind Jean-Léon Gérôme's painting _The Truth Coming Out of the Well_ (1896).

The Truth and the Lie go bathing together in a well. The Lie climbs out first, puts on the Truth's clothes and runs away. When the naked Truth climbs out to chase her, the world turns away in contempt. The Truth goes back into the well, ashamed – and ever since, the Lie has travelled the world dressed as the Truth, because the world has no wish to meet the naked Truth.

In a post-truth age, one of civil society's jobs is to help people face the naked truth – and to tell stories strong enough to compete with the well-dressed lie.

</Sidenote>

## From projects to overall transformation

The way of describing overall transformation I find most useful is this:

<Highlight>Addressing the <em>root causes</em> of issues, rather than only the symptoms, by helping structures, customs, mindsets, relationships and practices evolve – and by strengthening collective effort, with diverse people and organisations working together.</Highlight>

A framework from Aruna Rao and David Kelleher (2005) helped make this concrete. Change happens in four places: inside individuals (attitudes, values and practices), in individuals' access to resources, in informal culture (social norms and beliefs) and in formal institutions (policies, programmes and budgets). Most projects work in one corner. Lasting change needs all four – which is exactly why **collective action matters**: it can shift both individual lives and wider society, in both formal and informal spaces.

Most organisations work in more than one zone – and drift between them without ever deciding to. Naming the zone is half the strategy.

## The toolkit for lasting change

Lasting, overall transformation needs a deliberate mix of capabilities, and most organisations combine several:

- **Research and data** to influence narratives and decisions.
- **Demonstration** programmes that prove new ideas work.
- **Policy dialogue** to share evidence and model practices with decision-makers.
- **Capacity building** inside institutions.
- **Leadership development** of people close to the problem.
- **Coalition building** across individuals, networks and organisations.
- **Community mobilisation** to build people's participation.
- **Narrative**: using stories to change norms and beliefs.
- **Advocacy and partnership** with government and other institutions.
- **Big-picture analysis** to make sense of how the pieces fit together.

A useful exercise is to mark which of these your organisation is genuinely strong in, which you borrow from partners, and which are missing entirely.

## Collective action needs a new kind of leadership

A passage I read recently put it well. More and more, the successful parts of civil society are **not organisational at all**. They are fluid groupings of people and places – connecting, engaging, inspiring. Collective efforts are not owned and rarely visibly led, but they are supported. Networks are not controlled, but they build the social capital that drives change.

<Photo image={saltMarch} alt="Mahatma Gandhi walking at the front of a crowd of marchers during the Salt March in 1930." caption="Gandhi and fellow marchers on the Salt March, 1930. Collective efforts are not owned and rarely visibly led – but they bring lasting change. Photo: public domain, via Wikimedia Commons." />

That raises real questions for leaders like me who run organisations: how do we support collective efforts without taking them over? How do we take part in networks in ways that build trust rather than erode it? Often the answer is not a new structure, but changes in our behaviour, in what we value and in how we learn.

<Sidenote icon="info" label="A story worth knowing" title="The three stonecutters">

A traveller asks three stonecutters what they are doing. The first says, "Can't you see? I'm cutting a stone." The second says, "I'm cutting this block so it's square and fits exactly in its place in a wall." The third, the happiest of the three, says: "I am building a cathedral."

Overall transformation needs all three – the skill, the precision and the bigger picture. But teams only keep going through the hard years when they can see the cathedral.

</Sidenote>

## Strategy as craft

I keep coming back to Henry Mintzberg's idea that **strategy-making is a matter of crafting**: the ability to bring together the issues, people and events at hand into an integrated, meaningful whole. The craft draws on personalising the issue, paying attention, imagining, serious play and dialogue across boundaries.

An old text on the making of a _sthapati_ – a master architect – put the same idea differently. Knowing the theory is not enough, and neither is skill with the tools. What marks a true master is immediate intuition, readiness of judgement when things change, and the ability to fit every part into the needs of the whole.

That feels like the right description of leadership for lasting change.

## Questions worth asking

**For yourself**

- Are you building a cathedral, or cutting stones – and does your team know which?
- How do you support collective efforts without trying to own them?

**For your team**

- Which zone do you work from – delivery, demonstration, demand, or a deliberate mix?
- Which of these capabilities do you have, which do you borrow, and which are missing?
- Which root cause are your projects actually changing?
