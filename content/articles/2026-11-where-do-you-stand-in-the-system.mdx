_This is the fourth of five reflections from the "Broader Sectoral Perspective" module of the FMSF Leadership Development Programme, led by Gagan Sethi of Alliance for Change in February 2026._

Most of us in civil society are trained to deliver projects. We design them, run them, report on them and start the next one. This module asked a harder question: **is any of it changing the system that created the problem in the first place?**

## A world under strain

The module opened its look at the wider sector with a sober picture of what it called the 21st-century ecosystem:

- **Weakening institutional values.** Competition for resources is getting acute, and institutions – the market, the state and even civil society – are losing people's trust. The facilitator used the word _aitbaar_: trust itself is under strain.
- **Core values under stress.** Basic humanity – _insaniyat_ – is being pushed aside. "To be" has become "to have".
- **Conflict and inequality.** The paradox of poverty amid plenty, a growing sense of injustice, rising violence and shrinking space for people to be themselves.

<Sidenote icon="info" label="A story from the room" title="The truth coming out of the well">

To talk about misinformation, the facilitator shared a 19th-century legend behind Jean-Léon Gérôme's painting _The Truth Coming Out of the Well_ (1896).

The Truth and the Lie go bathing together in a well. The Lie climbs out first, puts on the Truth's clothes and runs away. When the naked Truth climbs out to chase her, the world turns away in contempt. The Truth goes back into the well, ashamed – and ever since, the Lie has travelled the world dressed as the Truth, because the world has no wish to meet the naked Truth.

In a post-truth age, one of civil society's jobs is to help people face the naked truth – and to tell stories strong enough to compete with the well-dressed lie.

</Sidenote>

## From projects to systems change

The definition of systems change we worked with was clear:

<Highlight>Confronting the <em>root causes</em> of issues, rather than symptoms, by transforming structures, customs, mindsets, power dynamics and policies – and by strengthening collective power through diverse people and organisations working together.</Highlight>

A framework from Aruna Rao and David Kelleher (2005) helped make this concrete. Change happens in four places: inside individuals (attitudes, values and practices), in individuals' access to resources, in informal culture (social norms and beliefs) and in formal systems (laws, policies and budgets). Most projects work in one corner. Lasting change needs all four – which is exactly why **movements matter**: they can shift both individual lives and whole systems, in both formal and informal spaces.

Most organisations work in more than one zone – and drift between them without ever deciding to. Naming the zone is half the strategy.

## The toolkit for systems change

Effective systems change, the module argued, needs a deliberate mix of capabilities, and most organisations combine several:

- **Research and data** to influence narratives and decisions.
- **Demonstration** programmes that prove new ideas work.
- **Policy development** to shape rules and model practices.
- **Capacity building** inside institutions.
- **Leadership development** of people close to the problem.
- **Coalition building** across individuals, networks and organisations.
- **Grassroots organising** to build people's power.
- **Narrative**: using stories to change norms and beliefs.
- **Direct lobbying** and, where needed, **litigation**.
- **Systems analysis** to make sense of the whole.

A useful exercise is to mark which of these your organisation is genuinely strong in, which you borrow from partners, and which are missing entirely.

## Movements need a new kind of leadership

A passage we read together put it well. More and more, the successful parts of civil society are **not organisational at all**. They are fluid groupings of people and places – connecting, engaging, challenging. Movements are not owned and rarely visibly led, but they are supported. Networks are not controlled, but they build the social capital that drives change.

That raises real questions for leaders like me who run organisations: how do we support movements without taking them over? How do we take part in networks in ways that build trust rather than erode it? Often the answer is not a new structure, but changes in our behaviour, in what we value and in how we learn.

<Sidenote icon="info" label="A story from the room" title="The three stonecutters">

A traveller asks three stonecutters what they are doing. The first says, "Can't you see? I'm cutting a stone." The second says, "I'm cutting this block so it's square and fits exactly in its place in a wall." The third, the happiest of the three, says: "I am building a cathedral."

Systems change needs all three – the skill, the precision and the bigger picture. But teams only keep going through the hard years when they can see the cathedral.

</Sidenote>

## Strategy as craft

The module ended this theme with Henry Mintzberg's idea that **strategy-making is a matter of crafting**: the ability to bring together the issues, people and events at hand into an integrated, meaningful whole. The craft draws on personalising the issue, paying attention, imagining, serious play and dialogue across boundaries.

An old text on the making of a _sthapati_ – a master architect – put the same idea differently. Knowing the theory is not enough, and neither is skill with the tools. What marks a true master is immediate intuition, readiness of judgement when things change, and the ability to fit every part into the needs of the whole.

That feels like the right description of systems leadership.

## Questions worth asking

**For yourself**

- Are you building a cathedral, or cutting stones – and does your team know which?
- How do you support movements without trying to own them?

**For your team**

- Which zone do you work from – delivery, demonstration, demand, or a deliberate mix?
- Which systems-change capabilities do you have, which do you borrow, and which are missing?
- Which root cause are your projects actually changing?
