Not long ago, a simple online dashboard or a small microsite for one of our programmes felt almost out of reach. We knew what we wanted to show. We just couldn't build it ourselves.

## Every feature had a price tag

Before AI, building anything online meant depending on someone who could code. We relied on their advice about what was possible and what wasn't, and we rarely had the knowledge to question it. A map, a filter, a page that updated with new results: each added feature cost us additional budget.

And it rarely started quickly. A website was a procurement exercise. We wrote terms of reference, went through the RFP route, compared quotes and waited. By the time a site went live, the programme had often moved on.

## Our stakeholders wanted to see the work

The frustration was that the people who cared most about our work wanted to connect with it directly. Partners, communities and supporters wanted real-time updates on what was happening – not just a few social media posts, and not only a report at the end of the year.

We were doing some great work on the ground. But we couldn't communicate it effectively, because doing so properly came at an enormous cost.

<Highlight tone="light" label="The gap">We were doing great work on the ground. We just couldn't <em>show it</em> – not because we had nothing to say, but because every page had a price.</Highlight>

## What changed with AI

With AI, we have been able to build what we need, quickly, and in the way we want it. Instead of handing a brief to an outside developer, we co-created the sites with our own staff team: the people who run the programmes, know the communities and hold the stories. AI did the heavy lifting on code, layout and first drafts. The team decided what the site should say, who it was for and how it should feel.

That is how we built our microsites. Here are two of them.

## Built on years of knowing what wasn't working

None of these sites came from a template. Each one is built on years of understanding our stakeholders' needs – and of seeing what didn't work in earlier sites, reports and updates.

That matters more than it sounds. When a technical person designs a site for you, the social side is often missed: who the people in the photos are and whether they consented, which words are respectful, what a parent, a funder or a young person needs to see first, and which stories should never be told in public. Those judgements come from the work, not from the code.

<Highlight>A developer can build you a website. Only the people who know the community can make it <em>tell the truth</em> about the work.</Highlight>

## What it saved – and what it opened up

- **Cost.** We now build and update sites for a fraction of what agency projects used to cost, and we no longer pay extra for each new feature.
- **Time.** A site can go from idea to first version in days rather than months, without a procurement cycle. Updates happen when there is news, not when there is budget.
- **Partnerships.** Each site is a ready window for future partners: a place to show results, explain the model and invite collaboration, without starting from scratch every time.

## Before you build your own

AI makes building a microsite far easier, but it doesn't make the decisions for you. A few things matter before you start:

1. **Know the purpose and the audience.** Decide who the site is for and the one thing each visitor should understand or do.
2. **Plan the logical flow.** Sketch the journey – from the first headline, to the story, to the evidence, to how to get involved – before anyone writes code.
3. **Understand hosting and domains.** Know where the site lives, who pays for the domain and hosting, when they renew and who holds the logins.
4. **Plan for updates.** "Real time" only works if someone owns the updates. Decide who updates what, and how often.
5. **Put consent and safeguarding first.** Check permission for every photo and story, take extra care with children and sensitive subjects, and keep personal data off the site.
6. **Check it works for everyone.** Test it on phones, on slow connections and for accessibility before you launch.

**What you should control:** your domain, your hosting account, the code, your content, consent records and the final decision to publish. **What AI can do for you:** draft layouts and code, write first versions of text, suggest structure, translate, and find and fix problems. Let AI do the building; keep the judgement – and the keys – with your team.
