Every year, tens of thousands of young people leave Madhesh. They board buses and flights to the Gulf, Malaysia and India – not because they lack ambition, but because ambition has nowhere to land at home.

That was the question in front of us in April 2026. SNV, FAO and Restless Development were coming together to build a **Centre of Excellence for Youth Entrepreneurship and Green Livelihoods** in Madhesh Province. We could have written its Charter in an office. Instead, we asked the young people it was meant for to write it with us – through a Youth Hack in Bardibas, Mahottari, which my colleague Shailendra Dongal and I led.

## What a Youth Hack is

A Youth Hack is a structured, youth-led design day. It borrows from design thinking, but it is built around one idea: young people are not an audience for solutions, they are the people best placed to design them.

The approach has a history. Restless Development has used Youth Power Hacks across Africa and Asia – including a series with Project Everyone and Unilever in 2020–21 that brought together young people from Zambia, Kenya, South Africa, Nigeria, Indonesia, India and the Philippines. In Madhesh we adapted it to one province, one economy and one question: _how can young people build a dignified, green livelihood at home?_

<Highlight>A consultation asks young people what they think. A Youth Hack asks them <em>what they would build</em> – and then gives them the day to build it.</Highlight>

## The day, step by step

We designed the day around five green sectors where Madhesh has real demand: agro-processing, renewable energy, waste and the circular economy, digital and IT work, and cultural tourism. Each group got one sector and one young person to design for.

## Why it works

**It keeps one real person in the room.** Every group designed for a named young person with a real story. Whenever a discussion drifted, the question came back: _does this help Asha? Does this help Imran?_ It is the simplest way I know to stop a workshop turning abstract.

**It moves from problems to roots to ideas, in that order.** Most planning jumps straight to solutions. The problem tree slows people down just enough to find causes they can actually change – a skills gap, missing tools, a system that does not reach them, a social norm.

**It treats energy as part of the method.** The cup wall, the balloon tower, the ball game after lunch: none of it was filler. A full day of hard thinking needs movement and laughter, and the games carried real lessons about vision, action and influence.

**It uses fair criteria.** Dot voting with clear tests meant the strongest ideas won, not the loudest voices – and that inclusion and climate were built into the choice from the start.

**It ends with people who can act.** Pitching to a panel that asks about feasibility and commitment turns a good conversation into the first step of a plan.

<Highlight tone="light" label="The facilitator's job">Hold the process tightly and the content loosely. The structure is ours; <em>the answers are theirs.</em></Highlight>

## What the day gave us

It was a full day of engagement, from the first bingo card to the last pitch. Each group left with ideas that had been tested, argued over and made real enough to present: posters, storyboards and business models built around a young person they now knew well.

But the part I keep coming back to is the connection. Young people who had arrived as strangers were working side by side by the afternoon, building on each other's ideas. That was not staged. It was real, and it is the kind of network a Centre of Excellence will depend on.

## From a day to a Charter

The Youth Hack did not sit in a report. What young people named that day – knowledge, service delivery, finance, mentorship and community support – became the **five pillars of the Charter** for the Centre of Excellence. The Charter says it plainly: its true authors are the young people of Madhesh.

That is why the day matters beyond the day. It laid the base for everything that follows. The young people who were there now see themselves as part of the process, because they are. They do not need to be persuaded that the Centre is for them – they helped design it, and they own it.

<Sidenote icon="plane" title="The flight where you might be asked to change seats">

Getting from Kathmandu to Madhesh is quickest by a short flight on a small propeller plane – and it came with a first for me.

Before take-off, the cabin crew asked some passengers to move seats for **‘load balance’**. On a small aircraft, where people sit really does change how the plane is balanced, so the crew spreads the weight across the cabin before it is safe to fly.

I have flown many times, but never been asked to move for the sake of the plane itself. It was a good reminder for the day ahead: sometimes everyone has to shift a little for the whole thing to fly.
