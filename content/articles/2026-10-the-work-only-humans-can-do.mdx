Indian civil society is changing faster than at any point in my working life. For generations it drew its identity from empowerment, care and charity – from *seva* and *parmaarth*, and from movements that gave voice to the voiceless. Today, launches and branding compete with the slow, unglamorous work of accompanying communities, and many organisations have grown hesitant and compliant, afraid of losing funding, registration or networks. On the FMSF Leadership Development Programme, I came to name this the **paradox of courage versus compliance**.

Into this unsettled sector, AI now arrives. It can feed the drift: cheap content and more fanfare, heavier digital reporting, and a wider gap between organisations that can afford the tools and grassroots groups that can't. Or it can do the opposite – take over the paperwork and give organisations back the time to listen, accompany and speak up.

My argument is simple. AI will take over much of the machinery of civil society: the reports, the budgets, the dashboards, the proposals. It will not take over the heart of it. The trust between a young person and a peer counsellor, a community's willingness to speak up, a team's belief in each other – these are still built one human encounter at a time.

## AI is arriving, but unevenly

In an international survey of 346 nonprofits, [92% were using AI tools in some form, but only 7% reported a major improvement](https://www.nonprofitpro.com/article/nonprofit-ai-adoption-hits-92-but-only-7-see-major-impact/) in what their organisation could do. Most use was ad hoc – one person, one prompt, one task – and nearly half had no AI policy at all.

In India, GivingTuesday's readiness study found that [55% of the organisations it surveyed had used generative AI, and 30% had never used AI at all](https://idronline.org/article/technology/are-indian-nonprofits-ready-for-ai/). And those respondents are mostly connected, mid-to-large nonprofits in cities. Most of Indian civil society is small and often rural, with modest budgets, no technology staff and unreliable internet. For them, this is less about current practice than about possibility and risk: what AI could offer, and what it could cost if the rest of the ecosystem moves on without them.

## Where AI is a genuine advantage

For a chronically under-resourced sector, AI's biggest gift is time. It handles volume, speed and structure, so people can spend their hours where only people make a difference.

| Function | What AI can take on | What stays human |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Project management | Workplans, timelines, risk logs, meeting notes | Judging when a plan must bend to what a community needs |
| Monitoring, evaluation and learning | Cleaning data, coding responses, spotting trends, drafting dashboards | Asking the right questions, and making sense of what the data means for people |
| Finance and compliance | Budget drafts, variance analysis, FCRA and CSR reporting, audit preparation | Accountability, integrity and the ethical calls behind every rupee |
| Fundraising | Donor research, proposal first drafts, tailored donor reports | A relationship with a funder, built on honesty and trust |
| Research and knowledge | Summarising policy, literature and evidence quickly | Lived experience and local knowledge no dataset holds |
| Communication and design | Social media posts, posters, short videos, translation into Indian languages, annual report layouts | Consent, dignity, and the honesty to show change as it really is |

Small organisations may gain the most: in the 2026 survey, those with fewer than 50 staff reported moderate impact more often than larger ones (41% vs 34%).

Communication is where the shift is most visible, and where it needs the most care. Work that once needed an agency is now within reach of a five-person organisation, which democratises voice. But in a sector already drifting towards fanfare, cheap content can mean more posts and less substance. And stories about communities carry ethical weight: AI-generated images of "beneficiaries" can misrepresent real people and strip them of dignity and consent.

> AI can write the post. Only we can earn the right to tell the story.

## What AI cannot replace

Picture civil society in the age of AI as three circles. At the centre is the human core, which AI can support but never replace. Around it is the machinery of our work, where AI is a growing advantage. And around both is the environment we live in: funding, regulation, markets, politics and technology.

- **Relationship.** Development is relational before it is technical. Community trust, built by turning up again and again, is the sector's deepest source of legitimacy, and it cannot be automated.
- **Emotion and empathy.** Feelings are how people decide whom to trust and whether to act. AI can mirror the language of empathy, but it sees patterns, not people.
- **Care and courage.** Care means taking responsibility for another, and sometimes taking a risk on their behalf. No efficiency gain can rescue an organisation whose care has run dry.
- **Values and conscience.** Respect, dignity and the courage to say no decide what we will not do, whatever the funding or the deadline.

The core is not fixed. Years of funding pressure, compliance and chasing quick wins can slowly reshape it until compromise feels normal – rarely in one decision, mostly in many small ones. So it needs tending.

**If a task needs presence, felt empathy or someone to be accountable, it stays human. If not, let AI help.**

## How to keep the core

1. **Name your non-negotiables, in writing**, and return to them whenever a tempting grant or partnership arrives.
2. **Hold regular soul checks**: honest conversations about whether we still know and live our values.
3. **Stay accountable downwards**, giving communities and young people a real voice in governance and feedback.
4. **Build the freedom to say no**, with diverse funding and modest reserves. Financial resilience makes courage affordable.
5. **Find courage in numbers**, through networks and alliances that let organisations speak together.
6. **Keep leaders close to the ground**, with time in the field and mentors and peers who tell the truth.

## The real risk: wider gaps

The risk is not that AI replaces civil society. It is that AI widens the gaps civil society exists to close.

- **A new digital divide.** In South Asia, [women are 32% less likely than men to use mobile internet](https://www.gsma.com/gender-gap-2025/). As services and jobs move online, those left offline – many of them rural women, Dalit and Adivasi communities and people with disabilities – fall further behind.
- **Bias built into systems.** AI learns from data that reflects exclusions of caste, gender, language and geography, and can automate discrimination at scale.
- **Data and consent.** The communities we work with hold the least power over their own data. We must protect it as a trust, not treat it as an asset.
- **Work and livelihoods.** Automation threatens the entry-level jobs many skilling programmes still train young people for.
- **Truth and civic space.** Misinformation and deepfakes can erode the shared truth democracy depends on, and civil society's credibility with it.

## What this asks of leaders

The leadership task is to use AI to free up human capacity, never to replace human presence.

- **Automate the machinery, protect the encounter.** Put the time saved back into field presence, listening and accompaniment.
- **Govern before you scale.** Every organisation needs a simple AI policy: what data goes in, who reviews outputs, what stays off-limits.
- **Put communities in the loop**, and build AI skills across the team, not just at the top.
- **Share the infrastructure.** No small organisation can build ethical AI alone: pool tools, data standards and advocacy.
- **Spend the dividend on courage** – on advocacy and standing with communities, not on more events and launches.
- **Invest in the inner edge.** As technical skills get cheaper, emotional intelligence, self-awareness and values become a leader's real advantage.

## What I'm taking with me

I came into the programme expecting to sharpen my outer edge: strategy, stakeholders, networks. I leave convinced that the inner edge – convictions, emotions and values – matters more, and AI is the reason why. AI will make us vastly more efficient. Whether we become more effective, more just and more human depends on what we do with the time it gives back.

My own work is with young people. What changes a young person's life is rarely a perfect report. It is someone who noticed them, believed in them and stayed.

The future of civil society will not be decided by who uses AI first. It will be decided by who stays most human while using it.
