_This is the third of five reflections from the "Broader Sectoral Perspective" module of the FMSF Leadership Development Programme, led by Gagan Sethi of Alliance for Change in February 2026._

Every leader I know carries a list of problems they can never quite fix. Should we grow, or go deeper? Should we follow the donor's priorities, or our communities'? Should we keep the founder's vision, or share leadership more widely?

One of the most freeing ideas from this module was that **many of these are not problems at all.** They are polarities – and they need a different kind of leadership.

## What a polarity is

A polarity is a pair of **seemingly opposite but equally necessary truths**. They are interdependent values that need each other over time. You cannot choose one and drop the other, and you cannot solve them once and for all. They are energy systems with a predictable pattern – and that pattern can be managed.

<Highlight>A problem has a solution. A polarity has <em>two right answers that need each other</em> – and a leader's job is to hold both.</Highlight>

## The polarities civil society leaders live with

The module listed the polarities that emerging leaders in civil society have to negotiate. Every one of them was familiar:

- **Global vs local priorities**
- **Scale vs depth**
- **Advocacy vs compliance**
- **Founder vision vs collective leadership**
- **Short-term survival vs long-term transformation**
- **Tradition vs innovation**

Inside organisations, the same pattern shows up in the classic tensions of any system: **differentiation and integration**, **centralisation and decentralisation**, **bureaucracy and entrepreneurship**, **tight boundaries and openness to the environment**, and **quality and innovation**.

And for those of us who work with young people, there is a whole further set. Among those we discussed were education versus employability, traditional social norms versus modern aspirations, independence versus family expectations, social media connection versus isolation, and a huge youth population versus very little political representation. Young people are also taught to respect elders while wanting to question authority, and to value humility while being told to be ambitious.

## Reflective practice: the skill underneath

Managing polarities depends on a deeper habit: **reflection.** The module drew on John Dewey and Donald Schön to describe reflective practice as stopping to think about our practice, analysing our decisions, drawing on theory and evidence, and relating it back to what we do.

Schön's distinction is the one I use most:

- **Reflection-in-action:** the quick thinking we do while something is happening – noticing, asking why, and adjusting on the spot.
- **Reflection-on-action:** looking back afterwards – what caused the situation, what options were open, why we chose one and not another.

The module added a third: **reflection-for-action**, thinking ahead about what we will do next time. Reflection is not linear but cyclical. It changes how we understand a situation, helps us see it from other perspectives, and makes us scrutinise our own values and assumptions.

We also looked at practical models for structured reflection, including Gibbs' reflective cycle, Rolfe's "What? So what? Now what?" and Atkins and Murphy's model. They are simple enough to use in a team meeting after a hard week.

<Sidenote icon="info" label="Try this with your team" title="What? So what? Now what?">

After a difficult decision, take fifteen minutes with your team and ask three questions from Rolfe's model:

- **What?** What happened? What did we do?
- **So what?** What does it tell us – about the situation, about us, about the tension we were holding?
- **Now what?** What will we do differently, and what will we watch for?

It turns a frustrating week into learning – and it makes polarities visible before they become crises.

</Sidenote>

## Living with ambiguity

One general observation from the facilitator has stayed with me. We are so conditioned to judge everything as good or bad – including every part of ourselves – that it becomes exhausting. Polarity thinking offers a way out: light and dark are not opposites, but different expressions of the same thing.

For a leader, that means accepting that some discomfort is permanent. The goal is not to make the tension go away. It is to stay steady within it, and to keep learning.

## Common pitfalls

- **Picking a side.** Declaring that "depth matters more than scale" feels decisive, but the neglected side always comes back.
- **Swinging.** Lurching from one side to the other with every new director or donor wears people out.
- **Reflecting only in a crisis.** Reflection works best as a regular habit, not an emergency measure.
