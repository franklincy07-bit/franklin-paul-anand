Young people in marginalised, climate-vulnerable communities are already acting on the problems around them. What they lack is not capacity or creativity. It is access to timely, responsive and trusting support that recognises their leadership.

A youth fund can be that support. Across three phases with Restless Development in India, supported by the Porticus Foundation, we have learned what makes one work – and what turns it into just another grant programme. This guide is for anyone in a foundation, NGO, company or government team who wants to set one up.

<Highlight>A youth fund is not a funding mechanism with young people at the end of it. It is <em>infrastructure for shifting power</em> – with money as one of its tools.</Highlight>

## Before you start

Be honest about three things.

- **Why you want it.** If the aim is to show youth participation, run a consultation. If the aim is to put resources and decisions in young people's hands, a youth fund is the right tool.
- **What you can let go of.** Young people will set priorities you would not have chosen. You need room to take risks and adapt as you go.
- **How long you can stay.** The real value comes over phases. Our first phase proved the model; the second scaled it across a state; the third is making inclusion the standard.

## Step 1: Decide who decides

Start with governance, not money. Set up a **youth-led steering group** through open and inclusive recruitment, and give it real power: shaping the fund's strategy, writing the grant criteria and making decisions on who is funded.

This is not symbolic participation. It is shared governance. Train the group, support it, and then trust it.

## Step 2: Choose themes rooted in real lives

Pick a small number of themes young people already care about and can act on locally. In our pilot, we focused on **climate resilience and active citizenship**. Young people took that in directions no programme plan would have predicted – from a district-wide campaign against single-use plastic to games that teach climate science.

Keep the themes broad enough for local ideas, and specific enough that grants add up to something.

## Step 3: Anchor it in the community

Young leaders do better when they are not alone. Pair each one with a **community-embedded organisation** that acts as mentor and anchor: helping with local relationships, technical questions and the paperwork a young person should not have to face alone.

This is also how inclusion becomes real. Local organisations can reach young people at the margins – because of gender, geography, caste, ethnicity or disability – whom a national open call would miss.

## Step 4: Make applying easy

Most funds lose the young people they most want to reach at the application form. Make it simple:

- **Short, simple applications** in local languages.
- **Video proposals** accepted alongside written ones.
- **Registered and informal groups** both eligible.
- **Clear, quick decisions,** explained to everyone who applied.

## Step 5: Fund the journey, not just the project

A grant on its own rarely builds a leader. We designed the fund as a leadership journey with four parts.

## Step 6: Connect every project to the system

Youth-led action has the biggest effect when it pulls institutions in. Encourage grantees to work with the people who hold the levers – municipal bodies, water boards, agricultural training institutes and elected representatives – from the start.

In our second phase, young farmers in drought-prone northern Karnataka are co-designing climate-resilient farming pilots with district agricultural training institutes and government schemes. In Bengaluru, youth-led work on water drew in the municipality, the city's water board and the office of the local legislator.

<Sidenote icon="info" label="From the field" title="The hundred-year-old well">

In one of Bengaluru's largest informal settlements – home to more than 40,000 people with little reliable access to clean water – the old open wells had become dumping yards.

Young leaders from the community saw something else: a community well with the potential to solve a water problem. They faced every kind of barrier – no technical knowledge, little political will, people expecting payment, and a community where young voices were often discouraged.

With flexible funding, a local partner, a water expert and a well protection committee, the well was restored and **400+ households** gained access to safe water. Their story inspired other young people to revive a second well and set up a drinking water unit – now reaching **800+ homes** – and helped unlock **over INR 2 million** in public and corporate money.

</Sidenote>

## Step 7: Let it ripple

Good youth-led projects spread on their own if you make them visible. Invite young leaders to share their stories in schools, public forums and local government spaces. Turn their tools into things others can use.

In our pilot, a youth-led campaign against single-use plastic grew into a movement of young "Green Warriors", reaching more than 15,000 people and influencing district commitments. Game-based climate learning tools, built by young people, were picked up in schools and public events.

## Step 8: Measure inclusion, not just activity

Counting grants and participants is not enough. Ask whether the fund is actually reaching and empowering the young people it is meant for.

In our third phase, we are piloting an **Inclusion Scorecard**, co-created with young people and grassroots partners, which looks at four things:

1. **Accessibility:** can young people at the margins actually apply and take part?
2. **Representation:** do the grantees reflect the communities the fund serves?
3. **Leadership participation:** are young people making decisions, not just delivering activities?
4. **Systems influence:** is the work changing how institutions behave?

## Common pitfalls

- **A token youth panel.** If staff can overrule every decision, young people will notice – and stop investing their time.
- **A form only professionals can fill in.** Complex applications filter out exactly the young people you want to reach.
- **Funding only registered organisations.** Many of the most active youth groups are informal.
- **Money without mentoring.** A grant without support can leave a young leader exposed to risks they cannot manage alone.
- **Projects in isolation.** Without links to local institutions, good pilots stay small and end when the money does.
- **Measuring outputs only.** Track who is included and who decides, not just how many events took place.

<Highlight tone="light" label="The one rule">Trust young people with the decision, and stay with them while they carry it out.</Highlight>
