_This is the second of five reflections from the "Broader Sectoral Perspective" module of the FMSF Leadership Development Programme, led by Gagan Sethi of Alliance for Change in February 2026._

Civil society organisations like to think of themselves as the good guys. We exist to fight discrimination, exclusion and abuse of power. So it is uncomfortable to be told that our own organisations are full of the very things we fight.

That was one of the hardest – and most useful – messages of this module.

## The deep structure of institutions

Institutions, the facilitator argued, are **mirrors of the power dynamics of the wider society**. They carry the same inequalities, discriminations and biases, even when their whole purpose is to remove them.

These biases rarely show up in policies. They live in what he called the **deep structure** of an organisation: who gets heard in meetings, whose mistakes are forgiven, which work is valued and which is invisible, how decisions are really made. Very often that deep structure has a masculine culture built into it.

<Highlight>Our organisations are not outside the problem. They are <em>small versions of the society we are trying to change.</em></Highlight>

That is why the work of humanising institutions – and building new ones – never ends.

## Power over, power under, power with

To look at this honestly, the module gave us a simple vocabulary for power:

- **Power over:** control, authority, the ability to make others do things.
- **Power under:** the hidden power of resistance, withdrawal or quiet sabotage by people who feel powerless.
- **Power with:** shared power, built through trust and collaboration.

Alongside these sit the three emotions that keep unhealthy power in place: **fear, guilt and shame.** In most difficult moments in a team, at least one of the three is doing the work in the background.

## The drama triangle

The idea that changed how I look at conflict was Stephen Karpman's **drama triangle** (1968). It describes three roles people slip into under pressure:

  label="The three roles – and the way out"
  items={[
    { tag: "Role", title: "Persecutor", text: "Blames, controls and criticises: “It’s all your fault.”" },
    { tag: "Role", title: "Rescuer", text: "Fixes things for others, often unasked, and keeps them dependent: “Let me help you.”" },
    { tag: "Role", title: "Victim", text: "Feels helpless and hard done by: “Poor me.”" },
    { tag: "The way out", title: "Responsibility", text: "Noticing the pattern and taking responsibility for ourselves, instead of reacting." },
  ]}
/>

The insight, developed further by Lynne Forrest, is that **all three roles are faces of the victim.** Whichever role we start in, we end up as the victim – and every role produces a lose–lose–lose outcome. It shows up as frustration, resentment, guilt, anger and exhaustion.

The rescuer is the easiest role to slip into in development work: stepping in for a team member, a partner or a young person, solving the problem for them, and feeling good about it. But rescuing keeps people dependent, and it eventually turns into resentment on both sides.

The way off the triangle is not a technique. It is a choice: **becoming conscious of our patterns and taking responsibility for ourselves.** Only then can we change them.

## Accountability is not enough

The second half of this theme moved from individuals to organisational culture. A quote from Joe Tye's _All Hands on Deck_ framed it:

<Highlight tone="light" label="Joe Tye">People will not desert a leader, they will only desert a boss. People will not quit a mission, they will only quit a job. <em>People will not leave a team, they will only leave an organisation.</em></Highlight>

His distinction between **accountability** and **ownership** is one I keep coming back to. Accountability is motivation imposed from outside – feet held to the fire, rewards and punishments. Ownership is intrinsic. It does not need anyone else to hold you to it.

The list we worked through makes the point sharply:

| You can hold people accountable for… | …but not for |
| --- | --- |
| Complying with rules | Living the values |
| Showing up on time | Being emotionally present |
| Meeting budgets and deadlines | Thinking like an entrepreneur |
| Results and competence | Working with passion |
| Saying the right words | Asking the right questions |
| Their job description | Putting their heart into the work |
| Treating people with respect | Honouring people's dignity |

Everything on the right is what actually makes a mission-driven organisation work. None of it can be enforced. It can only be invited, modelled and protected.

<Sidenote icon="info" label="A story from the room" title="The two wolves">

An old Cherokee story was shared towards the end of the module. A grandfather tells his grandson that a battle goes on inside all of us, between two wolves. One is anger, envy, greed, arrogance, resentment and ego. The other is joy, peace, love, hope, humility, kindness and compassion.

"Which wolf wins?" asks the grandson.

"The one you feed."

Organisations have two wolves too. Fear, blame and control on one side; trust, ownership and care on the other. Our everyday leadership choices decide which one grows.

</Sidenote>

## What I am taking with me

- **Look for the deep structure.** Ask who is not speaking in meetings, whose work goes unnoticed and how decisions are really made – not just what the policy says.
- **Stop rescuing.** Support people to solve their own problems, even when it is slower.
- **Build ownership, not just accountability.** Systems and targets matter, but they are the floor, not the ceiling. The things that make people stay – meaning, trust, dignity – have to be earned.
