_Part 2 of 5 in a series of my reflections, drawing on Gagan Sethi's "Broader Sectoral Perspective" module (Alliance for Change) on the FMSF Leadership Development Programme, February 2026._

Civil society likes to think of itself as the good guys. We exist to fight discrimination and the abuse of power. So one of the hardest truths for us to accept is also the most useful one: **our own organisations are full of the very things we fight.**

## Our organisations mirror our society

Institutions carry the same inequalities and biases as the society around them – even when their purpose is to remove them. These biases rarely appear in policy. They live in the **deep structure**: who gets heard in meetings, whose mistakes are forgiven, whose work is invisible.

<Highlight>Our organisations are not outside the problem. They are <em>small versions of the society we are trying to change.</em></Highlight>

## Power over, power under, power with

A simple vocabulary helped me see it. **Power over** is control. **Power under** is the quiet resistance of people who feel powerless. **Power with** is shared power, built on trust. And holding unhealthy power in place are three emotions: **fear, guilt and shame.**

In most difficult moments in a team, at least one of those three is doing the work in the background.

## The drama triangle

Stephen Karpman's drama triangle (1968) describes three roles we slip into under pressure. The twist, developed by Lynne Forrest, is that all three are faces of the victim – and every one of them ends in a loss.

<DramaTriangle caption="The drama triangle (Karpman, 1968). Whichever role we start in, we end up as the victim. The only way off is to take responsibility for ourselves." />

The rescuer is the easiest role to fall into in development work. Fixing things for a team member, a partner or a young person feels good. But it keeps people dependent, and in time it turns into resentment on both sides.

## Accountability is not enough

Joe Tye's distinction between **accountability** and **ownership** is the one I keep coming back to. Accountability is imposed from outside. Ownership comes from within.

You can hold people accountable for complying with rules, meeting deadlines and delivering results. You cannot hold them accountable for living the values, being emotionally present, working with passion or honouring people's dignity. Those are exactly what make a mission-driven organisation work – and they can only be invited, modelled and protected.

<Highlight tone="light" label="Joe Tye">People will not quit a mission, they will only quit a job. <em>People will not leave a team, they will only leave an organisation.</em></Highlight>

## Questions worth asking

**For yourself**

- When do you rescue, when you should be coaching?
- Which of fear, guilt or shame do you reach for under pressure?
- Which wolf are you feeding – fear and control, or trust and care?

**For your team**

- Where does the deep structure of your organisation contradict what you say you stand for?
- Who is not heard in your meetings – and why?
- Which of the things you cannot enforce – passion, care, dignity – are you actively protecting?

The question about the wolves comes from an old Cherokee story. A grandfather tells his grandson that two wolves fight inside each of us – one of anger, envy and ego, the other of kindness, hope and compassion.
