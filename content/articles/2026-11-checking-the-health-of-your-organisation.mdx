_Part 5 of 5 in a series of my reflections, drawing on Gagan Sethi's "Broader Sectoral Perspective" module (Alliance for Change) on the FMSF Leadership Development Programme, February 2026._

After looking at ourselves, at power and at the wider system, there is one question every leader eventually has to face: **how healthy is the place you lead?**

This guide brings together the tools we used. You can work through them alone, but they are far more powerful with your team.

## Step 1: Look at the whole organisation, not just the visible parts

Start with a picture of what an organisation is made of. I find it helps to see three layers:

- **What is visible:** programmes, strategy, impact and outcomes – the thinking, learning and doing space.
- **What is also visible:** structure, procedures, governance and accountability – the relating space.
- **What is invisible:** leadership and culture – values, celebrations, symbols, hopes, fears, informal networks and biases. This is the being space.

<Highlight>Most organisational reviews look only at the first two layers. <em>Most organisational problems live in the third.</em></Highlight>

Conflicts and polarities sit across all three, which is why they are so hard to fix with a new procedure alone.

## Step 2: Find your spark plugs, race horses, zombies and vampires

One of the most memorable tools I know is a set of four metaphors for the energy people bring to an organisation.

The exercise is to rate yourself, and then your team, out of 100 across the four. In most organisations, spark plugs are a small minority, and race horses make up the largest group.

Then come two honest questions, which I found more useful than the ratings:

- **What should I do, or stop doing, as a leader so that I am more of a spark plug – and so that others can be too?**
- **What internal compulsions or external pressures turn me into a zombie?**

Nobody is only one type. Most of us move between them depending on the day, the pressure and the culture around us.

## Step 3: Place your organisation on its life cycle

Ichak Adizes' **organisational life cycle** shows that organisations, like people, move through stages – and each stage has its own typical crisis:

<LifeCycle caption="The organisational life cycle, after Ichak Adizes. Each stage has its own typical crisis – the aim is to stay close to prime by renewing before the fire goes out." />

Knowing your stage helps you choose the right response. A go-go organisation needs structure; an aristocratic one needs its fire rekindled. Many civil society organisations sit between adolescence and prime, wrestling with exactly the founder vision versus collective leadership polarity from my third reflection.

## Step 4: Check your governance with the 5 Ps

A simple frame for institutional design is – the **5 Ps of governance**:

- **Purpose:** why we exist, what we do and when – the non-negotiable.
- **People:** who does the work and who decides.
- **Process:** how we work.
- **Programmes and projects:** what we actually deliver.
- **Policies and practices:** the rules – and whether practice matches them.

With one more added for civil society: **paisa** – money – as one marker of sustainability. Not the purpose, but a sign of whether the purpose can last.

## Step 5: Ask six questions over time

The last tool, drawn from Margaret Wheatley's work, was a set of questions about the **"arrow of time"**: is your organisation getting healthier or sicker as time goes on? Answer each one with evidence, not impressions.

1. **Quality of relationships.** Has trust increased or declined? Are people more self-protective, or more willing to go the extra mile for each other?
2. **Fear versus love.** Which is coming to dominate? Am I, as a leader, more fearful? Am I using fear to motivate people?
3. **Quality of thinking.** When a crisis happens, do we respond from our values? Is long-term thinking still happening in conversations, decisions and planning?
4. **Willingness to contribute.** What invitations to contribute have I extended, and how have people responded? Are they stepping forward more or less over time?
5. **The role of money.** How much do financial issues drive decisions? Has money become more of a motivator – for me, and for staff? Has self-interest replaced service?
6. **Learning from experience.** What have we stopped doing because it did not make sense? What have we started because of what we learned?

Wheatley's wider call is for leaders to create **"islands of sanity"**: places where people can do meaningful work together and bring out their generosity, creativity and kindness, even when the world around them is in turmoil. She asks leaders to reclaim leadership as a **noble profession** that creates possibility and humaneness.

## Common pitfalls

- **Rating others, not yourself.** The metaphors work only if leaders start with their own behaviour.
- **Blaming the zombies.** Zombie behaviour is usually a symptom of systems that drain energy, not a character flaw.
- **One-off health checks.** The arrow-of-time questions are most useful when you ask them every year and compare the answers.
- **Fixing structure when the problem is culture.** A new organogram rarely solves a problem that lives in the invisible layer.

## Questions worth asking

**For yourself**

- Are you more of a spark plug, a race horse, a zombie or a vampire this month – and why?
- What pressure turns you into a zombie?

**For your team**

- Where are you on the organisational life cycle, and what does that stage need from leadership?
- Over the last year, has trust in your organisation grown or shrunk – and what is your evidence?

## A closing thought on the five reflections

These five reflections have moved from the self, to power, to polarities, to the wider system, and finally to our own organisations. The thread running through all of them is a line from Dr B.R. Ambedkar: we have our liberty **in order to reform our social system**, which is full of inequality and discrimination.

Leadership in civil society is not only about running good organisations. It is about using whatever power and freedom we have to make the system fairer – starting with ourselves.
