## Monitoring and evaluation are not the same thing

We say "M&E" so often that the two words blur into one. They are related, but they do different jobs.

- **Monitoring** is the systematic, continuous collection and analysis of information about the progress of a project over time. The key words are _systematic_, _continuous_ and _analysis_.
- **Evaluation** is an assessment of performance against the project's goals and objectives at a particular point in time. It uses the information gathered through monitoring. The key words are _periodic_, _assessment_ and _judgement_.

<Highlight>Monitoring asks: <em>are we doing things right?</em> Evaluation asks: <em>are we doing the right things?</em></Highlight>

| | Monitoring | Evaluation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Timing | Continuous, ongoing | Periodic – mid-term, endline |
| Focus | Activities, outputs, short-term results | Outcomes, impact, relevance |
| Purpose | Is the project on track? | What is its value and effectiveness? |
| Nature | Descriptive | Analytical and judgemental |
| Main users | Project managers and staff | Funders, policymakers, managers |
| Data used | Routine data, activity reports | Monitoring data plus surveys and studies |

## Why monitoring matters during implementation

Monitoring is not just data collection – it is **a decision-support tool** that keeps programmes effective and accountable. It helps you to:

- **Track progress** – are activities happening as planned, and on time?
- **Identify problems early** – spot bottlenecks and risks before they grow into crises.
- **Ensure accountability** – give funders, government and communities evidence of what is happening.
- **Learn and adapt** – change course based on evidence, not assumptions.

<Sidenote icon="info" label="A scenario to think through" title="The vaccination gap">

You are managing a vaccination programme. Halfway through, the monthly data shows that vaccination rates are much lower in rural areas than in towns.

Good monitoring does several things here. It **detects the disparity** between groups. It **points to where action is needed**, so you can target effort rather than spread it thinly. It **informs decisions** and guides corrective action, and it **gives feedback** to managers and field workers as progress is measured against targets.

It also supports **equity**: without data broken down by area, the rural gap would be hidden inside a healthy-looking average.

</Sidenote>

## Six types of monitoring

Different types of monitoring answer different questions, and serve different decisions.

## A simple monitoring plan

A basic monitoring plan is a table with a row for each output you expect:

| Expected output | Activities | Target group | Monitoring tool | How often |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 500 students trained in life skills | Training sessions, group discussions, role plays | Students, grades 6–8 | Attendance sheets, pre and post tests, training reports | Every session; monthly |
| Teachers equipped with new methods | Training workshops, peer-learning circles, classroom visits | School teachers | Feedback forms, observation checklists, workshop reports | Per training; quarterly |

## Questions worth asking

Imagine a state government launches a scholarship for girls to reduce dropouts in secondary school, and your organisation oversees implementation in government schools. Work through these with your team:

1. What should be monitored regularly – and how often?
2. Who are the key users of the monitoring information?
3. How would this information help improve implementation?
4. Which monitoring tools and techniques would you use?
